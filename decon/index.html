Clinician Decon

Decontextualization is not de-identification.

One masks identifiers and takes the clinical meaning with them. The other removes the identifiers and keeps the medicine.

Open source · Local-first · Research release

Clinicians are already pasting clinical text into general AI tools. That is happening today, in every practice, with or without a policy about it. The useful question is not whether to stop it — it is what a physician should be able to paste.

Clinician Decon is a local-first tool that takes PHI-bearing clinical text and turns it into a reviewable, paste-ready prompt. You paste the text, it runs on your own machine, it shows you exactly what it removed or generalized, and you copy the safe version out yourself. Raw text is processed on 127.0.0.1. Nothing is sent anywhere, and the tool never puts prompt text into a third-party URL.

The distinction that matters

De-identification asks whether a document can be shared with identifiers removed. It is built for releasing notes and datasets. Decontextualization asks a narrower and more practical question: can this clinician safely ask a useful question right now?

The difference shows up immediately in what each one does to a clinical fact. A de-ID system is rewarded for emptying the text. A decon system is rewarded for keeping the text answerable.

Input contains Traditional de-ID might Decon aims to
DOB 3/15/2013 Mask the date Convert to adolescent
A1c 8.2 and BMI 31 Leave exact values, or mask them Convert to elevated A1c and obesity-range BMI when the exact numbers aren't needed
Addison Brooks has Addison disease Mask both instances of Addison Remove the patient name, preserve Addison disease
Only HLH patient on 7th floor today Leave the uniqueness context intact Convert to rare HLH case, drop the floor and the date
MRN LP-2024-08432 Mask with asterisks Replace with [MRN] and record only the removed category

The goal is not to prove a document is legally de-identified. The goal is to help a clinician build a safer, clinically useful prompt before using a tool that should never receive raw PHI.

What it removes, and what it protects

It strips the categories you'd expect — names, including relatives' and providers'; chart, claim, accession and device identifiers; phone numbers, emails, URLs and IP addresses; street addresses, ZIP-level geography, schools, camps, facilities, rooms, units, beds and floors; exact dates, relative dates, exact ages and birth dates. It also strips prompt-injection text, including instructions that try to talk the pipeline into preserving identifiers.

What it deliberately keeps is the part that makes the question worth asking:

  • Birth dates and exact ages become clinical age bands
  • Decision-relevant labs stay when triage or criteria depend on them
  • Exact weights stay when the answer is weight-based dosing
  • Broad relationship context stays, as in parent reports patient
  • Clinical eponyms and diagnoses stay, as long as they aren't functioning as identifiers

Three cases where the distinction bites

All examples below are synthetic. No real patient data appears on this page.

The eponym collision

A name-matching de-ID pass sees the string Addison twice and masks both. One of them was the diagnosis.

Input
Referral for Addison Brooks. Addison disease on fludrocortisone,
vomiting; stress-dose steroid?
Safe prompt
Referral for [NAME]. Addison disease on fludrocortisone,
vomiting; stress-dose steroid?

Uniqueness that isn't an identifier

No name, no MRN, no date — and still re-identifying, because the population it describes has one member.

Input
Only HLH patient on 7th floor today
Safe prompt
Rare HLH case

Safe, but not yet useful

A decon pass can succeed and still leave you with nothing worth sending. The tool says so rather than pretending otherwise.

Input
pt reports saw Dr. Thomas on 4/12/26
Safe review context
pt reports saw Dr. [NAME] on [DATE]

This is safe. It is also not a clinical question. The reviewer has to add one before this goes anywhere.

Why pediatrics makes it harder

Age is the whole problem. In adult medicine a birth date is an identifier you can usually drop without losing much. In pediatrics the age is the clinical variable — it drives dosing, the differential, the growth interpretation, and the red flags. A de-ID pass that masks the date and moves on has removed the thing the question depended on. That's why the age-band conversion sits at the center of this rather than at the edge.

The same is true for weight, which is both quasi-identifying and the input to most pediatric dosing, and for the family and household context that adult tooling treats as incidental.

Honest limits

What this is not

Clinician Decon is not a legal de-identification certification tool, and using it is not a HIPAA compliance determination. It is a local PHI-minimizing prompt builder with a human review step that is not optional. The review step is the safety mechanism — the pipeline is what makes review fast enough to actually happen.

It is a research release. There is no formal threat model behind it yet, no independent security review, and no production hardening. Evaluation to date has run on synthetic fixtures and adversarial sets built specifically to break it, which is the right way to develop it and is not the same thing as proving it safe in a live clinic.

If you are building something similar, the most useful thing I can tell you is that the failure mode is not the identifiers you remembered to strip. It is the context that identifies without being an identifier, and the clinical facts you destroyed on the way past it.
